Frequently asked questions

Is my audio uploaded to a server?

No. Everything — decoding, the waveform, the bleep render, the WAV export — happens in your browser with the Web Audio API.

What does the bleep sound like?

The classic TV censor beep: a 1000 Hz tone replacing the marked section, with tiny fades at the edges so there are no clicks.

Bleep or mute — which should I choose?

Bleep keeps the rhythm of speech obvious (great for comedy and YouTube-style censorship). Mute is subtler — better for redacting sensitive information.

What do I get when I download?

A 16-bit WAV file with your edits baked in, named yourfile-bleeped.wav. WAV is lossless and opens everywhere.

Can it find swear words automatically?

No — and that's the point. You mark each section by hand on the waveform, so there is no transcription, no waiting, no wrong guesses, and no audio ever leaving your device.

Bleep vs mute vs replace: choosing the right censorship

Three ways to remove audio, three different effects on the listener:

  • Bleep (1000 Hz tone) — the classic TV censor. It announces that something was removed, which is the point for comedy, YouTube, and broadcast: the audience knows a word was there and fills in the blank. Keeps speech rhythm intact.
  • Mute (silence) — subtler. Good for redacting names, addresses, or confidential details where you don't want to draw attention to the edit. On video, pair it with a cutaway so the silence doesn't feel like a glitch.
  • Replace (custom sound) — upload your own beep, quack, or airhorn. Creators use this for branding: a signature sound instead of the generic beep.

Rule of thumb: bleep when the fact of censorship is part of the joke or the format; mute when the goal is clean redaction nobody notices.

Getting clean marks: waveform reading basics

The hardest part of manual censoring isn't the tool — it's finding the exact word boundaries. Zoom in until you can see individual syllables: vowels show as dense, continuous waveforms; consonants (especially plosives like p, b, t) show as sharp spikes; sibilants (s, sh) look like fuzzy high-frequency noise.

Mark slightly wider than you think — about 30–50 ms of padding on each side. Too tight and you'll hear the ghost of the word's attack or tail; the tiny fades we apply at region edges prevent clicks but can't hide a half-bleeped syllable. Preview at full volume with headphones before downloading.

Why manual beats AI auto-detect

AI bleep tools transcribe your audio, guess which words are profanity, and bleep those timestamps. That pipeline has four failure modes manual marking doesn't:

  1. Language lock-in. Most auto-detect models cover English profanity lists. Marking by hand works in any language — Turkish, French, Arabic, invented Klingon insults.
  2. Context blindness. AI can't tell a slur from a reclaimed word in a lyric, or a medical term from a dirty joke. You can.
  3. Non-word sounds. Coughs, mic bumps, a name you promised to redact, a license plate read aloud — none are "profanity," all are bleepable by hand.
  4. Privacy. Transcription means your audio goes to a server (or a huge model downloads to your device). Here, nothing is transcribed because nothing leaves the browser.

The tradeoff is speed: AI scans an hour in seconds; manual marking takes as long as listening. For anything under ~10 minutes, manual is faster including correction time — AI timestamps always need human review anyway.

Podcast and YouTube workflow

Podcasters: record as usual, then do a dedicated censorship pass before mixing. Mark regions on the raw voice track, download the censored WAV, and continue your normal edit. Doing it pre-mix (rather than on the final stereo file) keeps music beds and ambience untouched.

YouTubers: YouTube's advertiser-friendly guidelines flag strong profanity in the first 30 seconds especially hard. Bleep early, bleep consistently — one unbleeped f-bomb in the intro can demonetize the whole video. Export the censored WAV and use it as your edit's audio master.

Teachers: make clean versions of songs for classroom use. Bleeped tracks keep the energy without the parent emails. Save the region list mentally — same song, same marks next semester.

Audio quality notes

Download is 16-bit WAV — lossless, universal, and the right choice as an intermediate. If your final destination is MP3/AAC (podcast hosts, YouTube upload), encode from this WAV rather than re-processing an already-compressed file; every lossy generation degrades quality.

The 1000 Hz bleep is a pure sine with short fades at the edges. It won't clip, it won't alias, and it sits in the frequency range where human hearing is most sensitive — which is exactly why it works as a censor. If you need a different tone for branding, the replace-audio option takes any sound you upload.

Frequently asked questions

How precise can the marks be?

Sample-accurate internally; practically, zoom lets you mark down to ~10 ms. The edge fades are a few milliseconds, so there's no click at boundaries. For word-level censoring, 30–50 ms of padding around the word is the sweet spot.

Can I bleep a live stream or a video file?

This tool processes audio files, not live streams. For video: extract the audio (or upload the video file — browsers can decode most video containers' audio tracks), censor it here, then mux the censored WAV back in your editor.

Is there a limit on file length or size?

Bounded by your device's memory, since everything runs locally. Hour-long podcasts work fine on desktop; very long files on low-RAM phones may struggle. If the waveform won't render, try a shorter segment.

Does it work offline?

After the first load, yes — the page is a static app and the Web Audio API needs no network. Your audio never touches a server at any point.

Why WAV and not MP3 download?

WAV is lossless and every editor opens it. Encoding to MP3 in-browser is possible but adds a quality generation for no benefit — encode to your delivery format once, from this WAV, in your normal toolchain.

Spotify Loudness: -14 LUFS Target

Spotify normalizes to -14 LUFS integrated, -1 dBTP. Learn Normal/Loud/Quiet modes, when to master louder than -14, and how to avoid loudness penalty.

Spotify normalization modes

Spotify uses 3 loudness modes: Normal (-14 LUFS) default on mobile/desktop, Loud (-11 LUFS) for noisy environments, Quiet (-19 LUFS). All apply loudness gain + limiter if needed, never extra compression.

Rule: If your track is -8 LUFS integrated, Spotify turns it down -6 dB to -14. You lose punch because your transients were already crushed. Mastering at -14 retains dynamics.

When to master louder than -14?

Club tracks, TikTok edits: -9 to -12 LUFS can still sound louder on Loud mode, because -11 target gives you 3 dB headroom. But on Normal mode they will be turned down.

True-peak for Spotify

Spotify recommends -1 dBTP, prefers -2 dBTP for lossy transcodes to Ogg Vorbis 96/160/320kbps. Inter-sample peaks +0.8 dB after transcoding cause distortion if you master at 0 dBFS.

Workflow

  • Measure integrated with JSM Loudness (verified -14.05)
  • Check short-term max < -9 LUFS for dynamic verses
  • Keep dBTP < -1
  • Export WAV, test Ogg preview

How Spotify's normalization actually works

Spotify measures your track's integrated LUFS once, when it's ingested, then stores a single gain value per track. At playback time it simply turns the volume up or down — there is no multiband compression, no "smart" mastering, no extra limiting on loud tracks. If your track measures -8 LUFS integrated, Spotify applies -6 dB of gain so it plays at -14. Your transients stay exactly as you left them; they're just quieter.

Quiet tracks get the opposite treatment, but with a safety net: if boosting a -20 LUFS track to -14 would push peaks over 0 dBFS, Spotify's limiter catches the overshoot. That limiter is transparent on most material, but it's still a limiter you didn't choose — another reason to deliver at a sensible level yourself.

One more detail people miss: normalization is per-track by default, not per-album. A quiet interlude followed by a loud single will each be normalized independently, which can wreck intentional album dynamics. Spotify Premium users can enable "Normalize volume" per-album in settings, but you can't count on it.

The three modes in detail

Normal (-14 LUFS) is the default on mobile, desktop, and web. This is the mode 90%+ of your listeners use, so it's the one to optimize for.

Loud (-11 LUFS) is designed for noisy environments — gym, car, street. Only 3 dB louder than Normal, but on a phone speaker in a loud room that 3 dB matters. If your track is already at -9 LUFS, Loud mode barely touches it, which is why club tracks and TikTok edits sometimes feel louder here.

Quiet (-19 LUFS) is for late-night listening and background music. Almost nobody masters for this mode, but it's worth knowing it exists: a -8 LUFS master gets turned down 11 dB here, and any pumping or distortion in your limiter becomes very audible at low playback levels.

What this means in practice: master for Normal mode (-14). Check your track in Loud mode mentally by asking "does it still hit at -11?" — if your integrated is -14 with healthy transients, the answer is usually yes.

Why -14 is a target, not a rule

The loudness war is over, but genre expectations aren't. A -14 LUFS master with PLR 12 sounds open and dynamic; a -9 LUFS master with PLR 7 sounds dense and aggressive. Both get normalized to the same perceived loudness on Spotify — but they don't sound the same, because normalization only matches average loudness, not punch, density, or transient impact.

So when is louder than -14 the right call? Electronic/club music (-9 to -11): the density is the aesthetic, and DJs need consistent energy. Hip-hop/trap (-10 to -12): 808s need weight that -14 can thin out. Pop (-11 to -13): competitive with references. Indie/acoustic/jazz (-13 to -16): dynamics are the point; let them breathe.

The mistake is mastering loud by accident — slamming a limiter because "louder sounds better" in the studio, then having Spotify turn it down 5 dB so it ends up quieter and more crushed than the dynamic version. If you master loud, do it deliberately, with intention, and check what it sounds like turned down.

True peak and the Ogg Vorbis problem

Spotify streams Ogg Vorbis at 96, 160, or 320 kbps depending on the user's quality setting. Lossy encoding creates new peaks that weren't in your WAV — typically +0.5 to +1.5 dB, occasionally more on transient-heavy material. A master peaking at exactly 0 dBFS can easily hit +1.2 dBTP after Ogg encoding, which means real clipping distortion on the listener's device.

Spotify's official recommendation is -1 dBTP, and -2 dBTP is the safer choice for loud masters or anything with sharp transients (drums, plucks, 808 slides). This costs you nothing audible — 1 dB of headroom is inaudible as a loudness difference — and it guarantees clean playback across all quality tiers.

Test it yourself: encode your master to Ogg Vorbis 160 kbps, decode it back, and measure true peak. If you see anything above -1 dBTP, your ceiling is too hot.

Podcasts on Spotify

Spoken word has different needs. Spotify doesn't publish a separate podcast loudness spec, but the industry convention is -16 LUFS integrated with LRA under 6 LU — slightly quieter than music, with tighter dynamics so speech stays intelligible in cars and on earbuds. True peak -1.5 dBTP is plenty; podcasts rarely have the transient content that causes Ogg overshoot.

If you publish both music and podcast content, don't use the same master chain. A podcast mastered like a pop song (-10 LUFS, crushed) sounds fatiguing over 40 minutes. A song mastered like a podcast (-16 LUFS, gentle) sounds weak next to references. Match the medium.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Mastering to -14.0 exactly and stopping there. ±1 LU is inaudible. -13.2 or -14.8 both fine. Don't chase the number; chase the sound.
  • Forgetting true peak. The #1 technical rejection cause. -1 dBTP minimum, -2 for safety.
  • Checking loudness on the MP3. Always measure the WAV you're delivering. Codecs lie.
  • Normalizing in your DAW after measuring. Any gain change after measurement invalidates it. Measure last.
  • Ignoring short-term. Integrated -14 with short-term constantly pinned at -8 means your verses are crushed even if the average looks right.

Frequently asked questions

Does Spotify really turn down loud songs?

Yes — any track louder than -14 LUFS integrated gets turned down by the difference, in Normal mode (the default). A -8 LUFS master plays 6 dB quieter than you exported it. It will sound exactly as loud as a -14 LUFS master, just with less dynamic punch.

Will my song sound worse if Spotify turns it down?

It won't gain distortion from the turn-down itself (it's just gain), but a heavily limited -8 LUFS master that's turned down to -14 retains all its squashed transients and pumping — it sounds smaller than a natively dynamic -14 master at the same playback loudness.

What about Spotify HiFi / lossless?

Lossless tiers don't change the loudness target (-14 LUFS still applies), but they remove the Ogg transcoding peak problem. True peak headroom still matters for the lossy tiers most listeners use, so keep -1 dBTP regardless.

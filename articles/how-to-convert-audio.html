How to Convert Audio Formats: MP3, WAV, M4A, FLAC

Need to convert audio from one format to another — MP3 to WAV, WAV to MP3, M4A to MP3, or FLAC to MP3? This guide covers which audio format converter settings to use, when conversion helps, when it silently destroys quality, and how to convert audio online free without uploading your files anywhere.

Audio format conversion is one of the most-searched audio tasks, and also one of the most misunderstood. Most online converters happily let you turn a 128 kbps MP3 into a "lossless" WAV — which does nothing except inflate the file tenfold. Before you convert anything, it pays to understand what each format actually is.

Why convert audio formats?

There are four legitimate reasons to run an audio file through a format converter:

  • Compatibility. Your car stereo plays MP3 but not FLAC. Your video editor wants WAV. An old sampler only reads AIFF. Conversion solves the "won't open" problem.
  • File size. A 5-minute WAV is ~50 MB; the same song as 320 kbps MP3 is ~12 MB; as Opus it's ~7 MB. For sharing, streaming, or phone storage, smaller wins.
  • Editing workflow. Lossy formats like MP3 are bad intermediates — every save re-encodes and degrades. Convert to WAV or FLAC before serious editing, then export the final master once.
  • Archiving. Keep masters as WAV or FLAC (bit-perfect), distribute as MP3/AAC/Opus. Never archive in MP3 if you can avoid it.

What conversion does not do: improve quality. Keep that in mind for the MP3-to-WAV section below — it's the single most common misconception in audio.

MP3 to WAV: what actually happens

Converting MP3 to WAV is the most requested conversion — and the most pointless for quality. Here's why: MP3 encoding permanently discards audio data (masked frequencies, fine detail above ~16 kHz at lower bitrates). That data is gone. Decoding to WAV reconstructs a waveform from what's left, but it cannot invent the discarded information. The WAV will be bit-identical in sound to the MP3, just ~10x larger.

When MP3 → WAV makes sense

  • Your DAW, sampler, or video editor refuses MP3 and demands WAV.
  • You're about to edit/process the audio — WAV avoids a second lossy encode.
  • A distribution platform requires WAV uploads (many do, e.g. DistroKid).

When it doesn't: "to make it sound better," "to make it lossless," or before uploading to a streaming service that will just re-encode it anyway. If anyone sells you an "MP3 to HD WAV enhancer," walk away.

WAV to MP3: choosing the right bitrate

This is the conversion that matters most, because bitrate choice is audible. MP3 at 320 kbps CBR is transparent (indistinguishable from WAV) for the vast majority of listeners on most material. The differences live at the low end:

BitrateQualityUse when
320 kbpsTransparentMusic distribution, DJ sets, anything you care about
256 kbpsNear-transparentGood balance for large libraries

128 kbpsAudible artifactsOnly for voice memos or extreme size constraints
V0 VBRTransparent (~245k avg)Best size/quality trade — recommended default

One more honest note: converting WAV to MP3 is a one-way street. Keep the WAV. If you later need AAC, Opus, or a different MP3 bitrate, encode from the WAV — never from the MP3. Each lossy generation stacks artifacts.

Convert M4A to MP3 and FLAC to MP3

M4A to MP3 is a compatibility move: M4A files (usually AAC) from Apple Music, iTunes, or iPhone recordings need to play on devices that only speak MP3. Technically this is transcoding — AAC already threw away data, and MP3 encoding throws away a little more. At 256–320 kbps the added loss is inaudible to nearly everyone, but avoid chains like AAC → MP3 → AAC. If you have the original CD or WAV, convert from that instead.

FLAC to MP3 is the clean case: FLAC is lossless, so you're encoding from a perfect source — exactly like WAV to MP3. This is how most people build their portable/phone libraries from an archival FLAC collection. Same bitrate guidance applies: 320k or V0 for music.

Going the other direction — MP3 to FLAC — is as pointless as MP3 to WAV. FLAC will faithfully preserve every MP3 artifact in a bigger file. Don't.

Does converting audio lose quality?

It depends entirely on the direction:

  • Lossless → lossy (WAV→MP3, FLAC→AAC): loses data by design, but at high bitrates it's inaudible. This is normal and fine.
  • Lossy → lossless (MP3→WAV, M4A→FLAC): loses nothing further, gains nothing. File gets bigger, sound stays identical.
  • Lossy → lossy (M4A→MP3, MP3→OGG): loses quality twice. Minimize this — always convert from the best source you have.
  • Lossless → lossless (WAV→FLAC, FLAC→WAV): bit-perfect. Zero loss, fully reversible.

The golden rule

Always convert from the highest-quality source you own, and convert once. A WAV → MP3 at 320k beats an M4A → MP3 at 320k every time, because the second one was already lossy.

Batch conversion tips

  • Convert once, from the best source. Gather all originals (WAV/FLAC) first; don't chain conversions.
  • Keep a consistent target. Pick one delivery format per purpose (e.g. 320k MP3 for the DJ USB, Opus for the podcast feed) rather than converting ad hoc each time.
  • Check loudness after converting. Format changes don't change loudness, but if you're converting and normalizing for Spotify or YouTube, normalize to -14 LUFS as a separate deliberate step.
  • Name outputs clearly: track-320mp3.mp3 beats track(1).mp3 when you're managing a library.

Frequently asked questions

Does converting MP3 to WAV improve quality?

No. Converting MP3 to WAV never improves quality. The MP3 encoder already discarded data permanently during compression — wrapping the same audio in a WAV container just makes the file ~10x larger with identical sound. Convert MP3 to WAV only when software requires a WAV file (some DAWs, samplers, video editors), never for quality.

What bitrate should I use when converting WAV to MP3?

Use 320 kbps CBR for music you care about, or V0 VBR (~245 kbps average) which is transparent for most listeners at a smaller size. 192 kbps is acceptable for voice, podcasts, and casual listening. Avoid 128 kbps for music — cymbal smearing and stereo collapse are audible. If the MP3 is for further editing, don't convert at all; keep the WAV.

Is it safe to convert M4A to MP3?

Converting M4A (AAC) to MP3 is safe but involves transcoding: AAC is already lossy, and re-encoding to MP3 discards more data. At 256–320 kbps the loss is inaudible to most listeners, but avoid repeated conversions (AAC → MP3 → AAC). If you have the choice, convert from a lossless original (WAV/FLAC) instead of from M4A.
